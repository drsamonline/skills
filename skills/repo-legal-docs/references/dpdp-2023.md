# DPDP Act 2023 and DPDP Rules 2025 — what a repository must do

Compiled from the Act and Rules, cross-checked against two independent open corpora (the DPDPA skill in `awesome-legal-skills`, last verified 2026-07-03, and the India jurisdiction rules in `legal-ai-skills`). Verify against India Code and the eGazette before publication.

## Status and dates

| Fact | Value |
| --- | --- |
| Act | Digital Personal Data Protection Act, 2023 — assent 11 August 2023 |
| Rules | Digital Personal Data Protection Rules, 2025 — notified 13 November 2025, 23 rules |
| Board | Data Protection Board of India, operational from 13 November 2025 |
| Full substantive compliance | 13 May 2027 (18-month transition from notification) |
| Appeals | TDSAT |
| Administered by | MeitY |
| Maximum penalty | ₹250 crore (s.33, Schedule) |

Treat the transition as a design deadline, not a grace period: notice wording, consent capture, age-gating, retention and breach pipelines are code changes.

## Vocabulary — use these words, not GDPR's

**Data Fiduciary** (not controller) · **Data Principal** (not data subject) · **Data Processor** · **Significant Data Fiduciary (SDF)** · **Data Protection Board** (not supervisory authority). Map the GDPR term once for a GDPR-fluent reader, then switch.

## Scope

- Digital personal data only — data in digital form, or non-digital data later digitised. Paper records never digitised are outside.
- Applies to processing within India, and to processing outside India relating to offering goods or services to individuals located in India at the time of collection (s.3).
- **Two lawful bases only**: consent (s.6), or a closed list of legitimate uses (s.7 — including medical emergency, epidemic, and certain employment and state functions). There is no legitimate-interests balancing test. If neither base fits, the processing is unlawful however reasonable it looks.

## Obligations that shape a repository

| Obligation | Where | What it means in code and docs |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Give notice before or with consent | s.5; Rules 2025 r.3 | Standalone, plain-language notice; not buried in T&Cs |
| Valid consent | s.6; r.4 | Clear affirmative action, unbundled per purpose, withdrawable as easily as given |
| General obligations | s.8 | Accuracy, completeness, security safeguards (s.8(5)), breach notification to the Board and to affected Data Principals (s.8(6)) |
| Children's data | s.9; rr.10–12 | Verifiable parental consent; no tracking, behavioural monitoring or targeted advertising directed at children |
| Significant Data Fiduciary | s.10; rr.13–15 | Only on designation — DPIA, audit, DPO in India, algorithmic due-diligence |
| Data Principal rights | ss.11–14 | Access, correction and erasure, grievance redressal, nomination. Publish the mechanism and a real response window |
| Retention and erasure | s.8(7); rr.8–9 | Erase on withdrawal or purpose fulfilment unless law requires retention; state the schedule |
| Cross-border transfer | s.16 | Transfer permitted subject to Central Government restrictions by notification — do not assert free transfer |
| Breach reporting | s.8(6); r.6 | Notify the Board without delay, and Data Principals; the Rule 6 window is 72 hours to the Board — confirm the notified text |
| Processor contracts | r.16 | Mandatory terms in a DPA with every processor |
| Grievance | s.13 | Named contact, published response timeline, escalation to the Board |

## Rule 3 notice — the nine elements

A privacy notice that will be tested must state, in plain language and as a standalone document:

1. Identity and contact details of the Data Fiduciary.
2. The categories of personal data collected, described plainly.
3. Each specified purpose, stated separately — "business operations" fails.
4. A brief description of the processing for each purpose.
5. Categories of recipients, including processors and other fiduciaries.
6. Retention period, or the criteria that determine it.
7. A summary of rights under ss.11–14 and how to exercise each.
8. The grievance mechanism, and the route to the Board.
9. How to withdraw consent, and what happens to data afterwards.

Plus: retrievable at any time, no more complex than necessary, English by default and available on request in any Eighth Schedule language.

## Health data — the case that matters most

Health data is sensitive personal data under the SPDI Rules 2011 (Rule 3) and personal data under the DPDP Act. A hospital or clinic management system is a Data Fiduciary holding data of identified patients, including mental and physiological health condition, and is very likely an SDF candidate once designations begin.

Minimum document set: a DPDP-grade privacy notice with the nine elements; a retention and erasure schedule per record class; a breach procedure naming who notifies the Board and how patients are told; role-based access described in plain words; a processor list (any hosting, backup, SMS or AI provider); and a statement of where the data physically resides, since a LAN-only deployment is a materially different privacy story from a cloud one.

## What not to write

- "We are DPDP compliant" — a conclusion you cannot evidence and a claim that ages badly. Describe behaviour instead.
- "We comply with GDPR and DPDP" when no DPIA, DPO or transfer mechanism exists.
- "Data is fully secure" — s.8(5) requires reasonable safeguards; no system is fully secure and the claim invites a consumer-protection problem as well.
- Any retention period you have not implemented in code.
